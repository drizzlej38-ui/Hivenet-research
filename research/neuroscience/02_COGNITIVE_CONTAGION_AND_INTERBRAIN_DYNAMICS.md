# Cognitive Contagion and Interbrain Dynamics

**Document type:** Literature-grounded research map  
**Status:** Preliminary; requires systematic review  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 2026-11-16

## 1. Research question

How do ideas, beliefs, emotions, attention, behaviors, and learned interpretations propagate between people, and under what conditions do social interactions produce measurable changes in individual cognition or coordinated neural activity?

“Cognitive contagion” is used here as a broad organizing phrase, not as one universally standardized mechanism. Research should name the specific phenomenon being studied.

## 2. Related established research areas

- **Emotional contagion:** changes in one person's emotional state associated with exposure to another person's emotional expression or state.
- **Behavioral mimicry:** automatic or deliberate imitation of gestures, expressions, speech patterns, or actions.
- **Social learning:** acquiring information or behavior by observing or interacting with others.
- **Cultural transmission:** propagation of information, practices, and norms across people and generations.
- **Information diffusion:** spread of messages or ideas through communication networks.
- **Group cognition:** cognitive work distributed across people, artifacts, and interaction.
- **Interbrain synchrony / interpersonal neural coupling:** statistical coordination between neural signals measured in interacting individuals.

These phenomena overlap but should not be treated as interchangeable. Neural synchrony does not by itself demonstrate thought transfer, shared subjective experience, or a causal influence on cognition.

## 3. Proposed multilevel map

A useful working model has three levels:

1. **Individual response:** a person attends to a stimulus and forms an emotional, cognitive, or behavioral response.
2. **Interpersonal influence:** communication, mimicry, shared attention, learning, and reciprocal adjustment influence another person.
3. **Network propagation:** repeated interaction and network structure can amplify, attenuate, transform, or block the information or behavior.

This is a research-organizing model, not a universal law. The 2025 narrative review “The Spread of Mind: Psychological Contagion in Theory and Critique” proposes a related Cascading-Resonance Model, but its authors characterize the synthesis as preliminary and call for causal, multilevel testing.

## 4. Neural mechanisms and measurement

Potential mechanisms and measurements include:
- Facial and vocal mimicry.
- Attention alignment and shared sensory input.
- Physiological co-regulation.
- EEG hyperscanning and interbrain synchrony.
- fNIRS or other simultaneous neural measurement.
- Behavioral coordination and learning outcomes.
- Memory retention and transfer.
- Network-level diffusion of messages or behaviors.

Interpretation requires controls for shared stimuli, common task timing, movement, measurement artifacts, prior similarity, and other confounds. Correlated neural signals are not proof that one brain directly transmitted a representation to another.

## 5. Relevant evidence

### 5.1 Psychological contagion framework

Houran, J., Dagnall, N., Sapkota, R. P., and Lange, R. (2025). “The spread of mind: psychological contagion in theory and critique.” *Frontiers in Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1631927

This narrative review organizes psychological contagion across individual resonance, interpersonal synchronization, and group-level cascade. It is useful as a conceptual map, but it is not an exhaustive meta-analysis and should not be treated as settled consensus.

### 5.2 Emotional mimicry and emotional contagion

Fu, Y. et al. (2025). “The shared neural substrates of emotional mimicry and emotional contagion: an activation likelihood estimation meta-analysis and meta-analytic connectivity modeling analysis.” *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*, 20(1), nsaf091. https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsaf091

This meta-analysis examines shared neural substrates of emotional mimicry and emotional contagion. It supports investigation of overlapping neural systems, not the claim that emotions or thoughts are literally transferred between brains.

### 5.3 Interbrain synchrony

Jiang, L., Zou, Q., and Yu, S. (2026). “EEG inter-brain synchrony in social interaction: A systematic review and meta-analysis of condition effects and outcome associations.” *Biological Psychology*, 210, 109367. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2026.109367

The review reports stronger EEG interbrain synchrony under social-interaction-related conditions and positive associations with external outcomes across the included studies. Heterogeneity in tasks, frequency bands, regions, and analysis methods limits simple generalization.

### 5.4 Moral-emotional diffusion online

Brady, W. et al. (2025). “Estimating the effect size of moral contagion in online networks: A pre-registered replication and meta-analysis.” *PNAS Nexus*. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf062

This work examines how moral-emotional language relates to message sharing in online networks. It concerns information diffusion and sharing behavior, not direct neural contagion.

## 6. Connection to learning and neuroplasticity

Social interaction can change what information a person encounters, attends to, rehearses, remembers, and practices. Those experiences may engage ordinary learning and neuroplasticity mechanisms. This does not mean social exposure automatically causes neurogenesis or that interbrain synchrony itself proves lasting neural change.

A useful research chain is:

social exposure → attention and interpretation → learning/practice → memory and behavioral change → possible neural adaptation.

Each arrow needs its own evidence. The chain is a hypothesis map, not a demonstrated universal causal sequence.

## 7. Research questions

1. Which forms of cognitive or emotional content spread most readily, and through which mechanisms?
2. How do social learning and emotional contagion interact?
3. Does interbrain synchrony predict learning after controlling for shared stimuli and behavioral coordination?
4. Which effects persist after the social interaction ends?
5. How do repeated exposure, network structure, trust, and source credibility alter propagation?
6. Can information spread while individuals retain distinct interpretations and independent identities?
7. How should causal effects be distinguished from homophily, common exposure, selection effects, and algorithmic amplification?

## 8. Implications for digital cognitive meshes

The analogy to a digital agent mesh should be used carefully. Digital systems can explicitly copy, label, route, and record information. Human social influence is embodied, context-dependent, and mediated by perception, memory, emotion, and relationships.

A digital mesh can model:
- Propagation paths.
- Source attribution.
- Exposure frequency.
- Independent interpretation.
- Reinforcement and decay.
- Divergence among recipients.
- Provenance-preserving knowledge exchange.

Such simulations can help formulate hypotheses, but they cannot by themselves establish biological or psychological causation.

## 9. Open questions and cautions

- “Cognitive contagion” is not a single accepted mechanism.
- Correlation between interacting brains is not direct brain-to-brain communication.
- Neural synchrony is sensitive to shared sensory input and analytic choices.
- Online information diffusion is not equivalent to internal cognitive adoption.
- Cultural transmission can preserve ideas without preserving the original individuals' neural states.
- Claims about unconscious transmission require operational definitions and appropriate controls.
